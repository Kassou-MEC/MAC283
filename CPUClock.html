The CPU as a Clock:
The Mathematics of Time

Forget the components for a lecture. A processor is not a box of parts — it is a metronome. Every register, ALU, bus, and cache marches to one drumbeat. Today we study that drumbeat with equations: period, CPI, execution time, speedup, pipelines, stalls, and power.

01 · Fundamentals

The Clock Signal: T = 1 / f

The clock is a square wave. One full high-low swing is a cycle. The time it takes is the period T; how many cycles fit in one second is the frequency f. They are reciprocals — know one, you know the other.

The clock is the CPU's heartbeat. Frequency f asks “how many beats per second?”; period T asks “how long is one beat?” Everything else in this lecture is just counting beats — so this reciprocal pair is the foundation of every formula that follows.
T= 1f   and   f= 1T
Period T
0.286 ns

At 3.5 GHz, one cycle lasts 0.286 ns. In that time, light itself only travels about 8.6 cm — roughly the width of your palm. This is why motherboard trace lengths matter and why a CPU cannot casually "ask" the RAM chip 10 cm away for data inside a single cycle. Distance is a real cost at these speeds.
02 · Units

Orders of Magnitude

Architecture math constantly hops between prefixes. Mixing up ns and ps is the #1 source of wrong answers on the exam. Anchor these.

The prefixes are just powers of 1000: kilo→mega→giga multiply, milli→micro→nano→pico divide. Handy pairing to memorize: GHz frequencies always produce ns/ps periods. If your answer comes out in whole seconds for one cycle, you dropped an exponent.

When we later compute stall cycles, remember this: to the CPU, memory is glacially slow.
03 · Sequential Timing (this ties to our flip-flop unit)

What Limits the Clock?

You built flip-flops and combinational logic earlier this semester. Between two flip-flops, a signal must launch, ripple through logic, and arrive before the next clock edge. The slowest such path — the critical path — sets the shortest legal period.

Think of two flip-flops as train stations and the signal as a train. The clock edge is the departure whistle at station A and the arrival deadline at station B. The tick can't be shorter than the slowest train's full trip — launch delay + track (logic) + arrival margin (setup) + timetable error (skew). Find the slowest path, take 1 over it, and that's the chip's maximum GHz.
TC≥ tpcq+ tlogic+ tsetup+ tskew   ⇒   fmax= 1TC
Minimum period TC
285 ps
Maximum frequency
3.51 GHz
Biggest contributor
t_logic

WHY PIPELINING WILL WORK

Notice: tlogic dominates. If we split the logic into shorter stages separated by registers, each stage's tlogic shrinks → TC shrinks → f rises. That is the entire mathematical motivation for pipelining (slide 10).

WHY OVERCLOCKING CRASHES

Raise f beyond fmax and the signal arrives after the capture edge — the flip-flop latches garbage. Overclockers raise voltage to make transistors switch faster (shrinking tlogic), buying back margin… at a power cost (slide 12).

The silicon lottery. Chips cut from the same wafer come out microscopically different — so each die's real tlogic differs. Intel and AMD test every chip's actual critical path and “bin” them: the lucky dies ship as the 5.7 GHz flagship at a premium, the slower siblings become mid-range SKUs. CPU pricing is literally critical-path statistics. Overclockers hunting a “golden sample” are hunting a die with an unusually short TC.
04 · The Master Equation

The Iron Law of Performance

One equation governs everything in this course. Memorize it, tattoo it on your brain — every performance question is this equation wearing a costume.

Total time = how many instructions × how many ticks each costs × how long a tick lasts. Three independent knobs owned by three different groups of engineers: your code and compiler own IC, the chip architect owns CPI, the circuit/physics team owns TC. Speeding up a computer means turning at least one knob without spoiling the others.
CPU Time= IC× CPI× TC  =  IC × CPIf

IC — INSTRUCTION COUNT

How many machine instructions the program executes. Set by the algorithm, compiler, and ISA — your assembly skills live here.

CPI — CYCLES PER INSTRUCTION

Average clock cycles each instruction costs. Set by the microarchitecture: pipelining, hazards, cache misses.

TC — CYCLE TIME

Seconds per cycle = 1/f. Set by the circuit technology and critical path from the previous slide.

Worked example — click to reveal each step

A program executes IC = 2 × 10⁹ instructions on a 3.2 GHz CPU with an average CPI = 1.25.
Cycles needed = IC × CPI = 2×10⁹ × 1.25 = 2.5 × 10⁹ cycles
CPU Time = cycles / f = 2.5×10⁹ / 3.2×10⁹ = 0.781 s
Sanity check with units: instructions × (cycles/instruction) × (seconds/cycle) = seconds ✓ — the units always tell you if your formula is assembled correctly.
A 60 fps game gives your code a frame budget of 16.67 ms. At 3.2 GHz that's 16.67×10⁻³ × 3.2×10⁹ ≈ 53 million cycles per frame. At CPI ≈ 1.25, that's ~42 million instructions to simulate physics, AI, and audio — every single frame. Game engine programmers (MAC280 students, take note) budget cycles like money.
05 · CPI in Detail

CPI Is a Weighted Average

Real programs mix instruction types, and each type costs different cycles. The overall CPI is the weighted average over the instruction mix — weights are the fraction of each class.

It works exactly like your GPA: each instruction class is a course, its cycle cost is the grade, and its share of the program is the credit weight. A program full of cheap ALU ops has a great “GPA” (low CPI); a load-heavy one drags the average down.
CPI= Σ(CPIi×fraci)  =  Total clock cyclesInstruction count

06 · Comparing Machines

Head-to-Head: Which CPU Wins?

Higher GHz does not mean faster. Only the full Iron Law decides. We compare with the speedup ratio:

To settle “which machine is faster,” compute each machine's total time with the Iron Law and divide. GHz alone only tells you how fast the metronome ticks — it says nothing about how much work gets done per tick. That's CPI's job.
Speedup= Timeold / slowerTimenew / faster

Case study — click through the calculation

Same compiled program, IC = 4 × 10⁹ on both machines. Machine A: 4.0 GHz, CPI = 2.0  ·  Machine B: 2.8 GHz, CPI = 1.2

TimeA = IC × CPI / f = 4×10⁹ × 2.0 / 4.0×10⁹ = 2.00 s
TimeB = 4×10⁹ × 1.2 / 2.8×10⁹ = 1.71 s
Speedup of B over A = 2.00 / 1.71 = 1.17× — the "slower" 2.8 GHz chip wins by 17%.
Lesson: B's efficient microarchitecture (CPI 1.2 vs 2.0) beats A's raw clock. Marketing sells GHz; engineers buy CPI × TC.
This exact plot twist happened in the mid-2000s: Intel's Pentium 4 chased 3.8 GHz with a deep pipeline and high effective CPI; AMD's Athlon 64 and later Intel's own Core 2 ran lower clocks with much lower CPI — and won decisively. Modern example: Apple's M-series runs ~3–4 GHz yet matches or beats 5+ GHz x86 desktops on many workloads by keeping CPI low (wide issue, huge caches).
07 · A Misleading Metric

The MIPS Trap

MIPS = Millions of Instructions Per Second. It sounds like a performance number. It is actually a marketing number — old-timers call it "Meaningless Indicator of Processor Speed."

MIPS counts instructions per second — but instructions are not equal units of work. Judging CPUs by MIPS is like judging authors by words typed per minute: a writer who says the same thing in fewer, richer words “scores” lower yet finishes the book first.
MIPS= ICTime × 10⁶ = fCPI × 10⁶

Why it lies: same task, two compilers, one machine (f = 2 GHz)

VersionICCPITime = IC×CPI/fMIPS = f/(CPI·10⁶)
Compiler 1 (simple instrs)10 × 10⁹1.05.0 s2000
Compiler 2 (complex instrs)4 × 10⁹2.04.0 s1000

THE VERDICT

Compiler 2 finishes a full second sooner (4.0 s vs 5.0 s) yet scores half the MIPS. MIPS counts instructions, not work — and instructions are not equal units of work across ISAs or compilers.

WHAT PROFESSIONALS USE

Execution time on real benchmarks (SPEC CPU, Geekbench) — because the only metric that never lies is the wall-clock time of the workload you actually care about.

In the 1980s–90s, vendors printed MIPS on ads, benchmarked against the DEC VAX-11/780 (“the 1-MIPS machine”) — and gamed it with instruction sets full of tiny, cheap ops that inflated the count. The modern echo: phone ads shouting “octa-core!” as if 8 × anything beats 6 × something faster per core. It's why the industry moved to workload benchmarks (SPEC, Geekbench, Cinebench): measure the time to do real work, not the count of steps taken.
08 · The Law of Diminishing Returns

Amdahl's Law

Suppose you speed up only part of a task — a fraction p of the time — by factor s. The overall speedup is capped by the part you did not improve.

Your commute is 10 min of city blocks + 50 min of highway. Buy a car that does the highway infinitely fast: the trip still takes 10 minutes. The untouched fraction (1−p) becomes the floor — that's the whole law in one sentence.
Speedupoverall= 1(1 − p) + p / s   with limit   11 − p as s → ∞
Overall speedup
3.33×
Ceiling (s → ∞)
5.00×
Time saved
70%
You buy a GPU that runs the parallel 80% of your ML training infinitely fast. Total speedup? Still only 5×, because the serial 20% (data loading, Python glue code) now dominates. This is why data-center engineers obsess over "the last serial 5%" — and why adding a 65th CPU core often buys you almost nothing. 09 · More Work Per Tick

Pipelining the Clock

Split instruction execution into k stages separated by registers. Each stage's logic is shorter → the cycle can be shorter (slide 4!) → and a new instruction can enter every cycle, like laundry: wash, dry, fold in parallel.

Pipelining doesn't make one instruction finish faster (its latency is the same or slightly worse) — it makes instructions finish more often (throughput → one per cycle). The clock gets faster because each stage's logic is shorter, and the hardware stays busy on 5 instructions at once.
Tpipe=max(tstage)+treg  ·  Cycles(n)=k + n − 1  ·  Speedupideal→k
Current cycle
0
Pipelined total
k+n−1 = 9 cycles
Non-pipelined
k×n = 25 cycles

Numbers with real timing

Single-cycle datapath: all logic in one cycle, T = 800 ps → f = 1.25 GHz, one instruction per 800 ps.
Split into 5 stages of 160 ps + 40 ps register overhead → Tpipe = 200 ps → f = 5 GHz.
For n = 10⁹ instructions: pipelined time ≈ (5 + 10⁹ − 1) × 200 ps ≈ 0.20 s vs single-cycle 0.80 s → speedup ≈ 4× (not 5× — the register overhead and fill cycles eat the difference).
Hazards (branches, load-use) insert stall cycles, so real CPI = 1 + stalls per instruction. Pipelining raises f; hazards fight back through CPI. Performance is always the tug-of-war between the two.
Ford's 1913 assembly line cut chassis build time from 12.5 hours to 93 minutes — not by making any worker faster, but by overlapping stages. Same math at the drive-thru: order → pay → pickup is a 3-stage pipeline. Each car spends ~3 minutes inside, yet one exits every ~45 seconds. Latency unchanged, throughput tripled — exactly what a 5-stage CPU pipeline does to instructions.
10 · When Memory Stalls the Clock

AMAT: The Cost of Missing

The clock keeps ticking whether or not data arrives. A cache hit costs a couple of cycles; a miss forces the CPU to wait out a long penalty. The expected cost per access:

This is the expected value formula from probability class: average cost = guaranteed cost + (probability of the bad event × price of the bad event). The CPU doesn't pause its clock during a miss — it burns real cycles waiting, and those wasted ticks show up as extra CPI.
AMAT= Hit time+ Miss rate× Miss penalty

Do the math on a 5% miss rate: only 1 access in 20 misses, yet with a 120-cycle penalty those rare misses cost 6 cycles on average vs 2 for all the hits — the rare event dominates the bill. This is the same math as insurance: low probability × huge cost = your biggest expense. It's also why doubling cache size (halving miss rate) often beats raising the clock 20%.
11 · Why Clocks Stopped Climbing

The Power Wall: P = αCV²f

Every clock tick charges and discharges billions of tiny capacitors. Dynamic power grows linearly with f — but pushing f up requires raising voltage V, and power grows with V squared. Net effect: power ≈ f³. Heat is the wall.

Each tick charges and drains billions of microscopic capacitors — that's physically what “computing” is. More ticks per second means more charging events; higher voltage (needed for faster ticks) is squared in the formula. Multiply them and the chip becomes a space heater. Physics, not imagination, is why clocks froze near 5 GHz.
Pdynamic= α× C× V²× f   (α: activity, C: switched capacitance)

Try: 6 GHz typically needs ≈1.4 V → power ≈ 4× — this is why 10 GHz never happened.

YOUR LAPTOP, RIGHT NOW

"Turbo boost" is this equation being solved live: the CPU sprints at high f/V until the temperature sensor says stop, then throttles f down. Thin laptops throttle sooner — the math is identical, the heatsink is smaller.

THE INDUSTRY'S ANSWER

Since ~2005, clocks plateaued near 3–5 GHz. Instead of faster ticks, we get more cores per tick — which hands the performance problem to software… and to Amdahl's Law from slide 9. The whole modern era of parallel programming exists because of V².

Data centers feel V²f on the electric bill: cooling can consume a large share of total power, which is why Google and Meta build near cheap hydropower and cold climates (Finland, Oregon) — and why performance-per-watt, not GHz, is the metric cloud providers buy on. On your side of the screen: leave your phone gaming in a hot car and the frame rate drops — that's the OS deliberately lowering f and V to solve P = αCV²f for a survivable temperature.

Q1 · A CPU runs at 2.5 GHz. What is its clock period?
T = 1/f = 1 / (2.5×10⁹) = 0.4×10⁻⁹ s = 0.4 ns. Tip: at "X GHz", period is simply (1/X) ns.
Q2 · Program: IC = 6×10⁹, CPI = 2, f = 4 GHz. Execution time?
Time = IC×CPI/f = (6×10⁹ × 2) / 4×10⁹ = 12/4 = 3.0 s.
Q3 · You speed up 50% of a program by 100×. Best possible overall speedup?
Amdahl: 1/((1−0.5)+0.5/100) = 1/0.505 ≈ 1.98×. The untouched half caps you at 2× no matter what.
Q4 · 5-stage pipeline, 200 instructions, no hazards. Total cycles?
k + n − 1 = 5 + 200 − 1 = 204 cycles. Non-pipelined would be 5×200 = 1000.
Q5 · Cache: hit = 1 cycle, miss rate = 10%, penalty = 50 cycles. AMAT?
AMAT = 1 + 0.10 × 50 = 6 cycles. One access in ten costing 50 cycles quintuples your average cost.
