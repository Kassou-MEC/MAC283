The Contract:
How Software Speaks to Silicon

Last lecture, the Iron Law gave you three knobs: IC × CPI × TC. Today we open the knob your code owns — IC. The Instruction Set Architecture is the contract between every program ever written and the metal that runs it: a fixed vocabulary of operations, registers, and formats. Learn the vocabulary, and assembly language is just reading the contract out loud.

01 · The Big Picture

The ISA Is a Contract

The ISA is the complete list of everything a programmer can ask the hardware to do: the instructions, the registers, the memory model, and the binary encodings. Above the line, software promises to only use this vocabulary. Below the line, hardware promises that any chip — any vendor, any year — will honor it identically.

Think of the ISA as a restaurant menu. You (the program) may order only what's printed on it. The kitchen (the microarchitecture) can reorganize, hire, buy new ovens — as long as every dish on the menu comes out exactly as described. That's why a program compiled in 2005 still runs on a 2026 x86 CPU: same menu, wildly different kitchen.
HIGH-LEVEL LANGUAGE — what you write
g = h + A[8];
▼ COMPILER
ASSEMBLY LANGUAGE — the ISA in human-readable form
lw $t0, 32($s3) add $s0, $s1, $t0
▼ ASSEMBLER

10001110011010000000000000100000 00000010001010001000000000100000

ABOVE THE CONTRACT

Algorithms, compilers, your assembly skills. Together they decide IC — how many instructions the program executes. This is the Iron Law knob we study today and for the rest of the semester.

BELOW THE CONTRACT

Pipelines, caches, branch predictors — the microarchitecture. It decides CPI, invisible to the contract. Two chips can run the identical ISA with totally different speed (remember Machine A vs Machine B last lecture).

The contract is a business asset. Intel and AMD have a decades-long cross-license just to legally share the x86 menu. Apple's 2020 Mac transition swapped the entire kitchen (x86 → ARM) and used Rosetta 2 to translate one menu into the other — a contract-to-contract translator. When you hear "M-series runs x86 apps," that's ISA translation, live, on your laptop.
02 · The Workbench

Registers: Zero-Distance Data

Last lecture: light travels 8.6 cm per cycle, and RAM is ~250 cycles away. The ISA's answer is a tiny set of storage cells inside the CPU — registers — reachable in zero extra cycles. MIPS gives you exactly 32 of them, each 32 bits wide. Every computation happens here.

Registers are the workbench; memory is the warehouse. A carpenter doesn't walk to the warehouse for every nail — they keep the tools in hand and make warehouse trips (loads/stores) as rare as possible. Great assembly programmers and great compilers are judged by how well they keep work on the bench.

WHY EXACTLY 32?

A register number must fit in an instruction field. 32 registers → 5 bits per register name (2⁵ = 32) — three of them fit comfortably inside one 32-bit instruction. More registers would mean wider fields, fatter instructions, bigger programs. This is the number-systems math from Week 1 making a billion-dollar design decision.

$zero is the strangest and most useful: reading it always yields 0, writing to it does nothing. Want a copy? add $t0, $s1, $zero. One wired-to-ground register replaces a whole family of special instructions.

03 · Anatomy

Dissecting an Instruction

Every MIPS instruction is exactly one 32-bit word. An arithmetic (R-format) instruction packs six fields: what to do (opcode + funct), where the inputs live (rs, rt), and where the answer goes (rd). Assembly language is just these fields with names instead of bit patterns.

Read add $t0, $s1, $s2 left to right as: "add, and the first register listed is the destination." So it means $t0 = $s1 + $s2. The destination-first rule trips up every beginner exactly once — let it be today, not on the exam.

04 · Formats

Three Formats Rule Them All

MIPS encodes its entire vocabulary in just three templates: R (register), I (immediate), and J (jump). Same width, same opcode position — so the decoder knows where to look before it even knows what the instruction is.

Three formats is a design promise to the hardware: "you will never have to guess where a field starts." Compare x86, where instructions run 1 to 15 bytes and the decoder must read a byte just to learn how many more to read. Regularity is what makes fast, pipelined decoding cheap — remember, shorter tlogic → shorter TC → higher f.

THE 16-BIT CATCH

An I-format immediate is 16 bits of two's complement — range −32,768 … +32,767. Need a bigger constant? The assembler quietly splits it into two instructions (lui + ori). Your two's-complement unit wasn't a warm-up — it's load-bearing.

DESIGN PRINCIPLE

Regularity enables speed. rs and rt sit at the same bit positions in R and I formats, so the register file can be read before decoding finishes — a trick the pipelined datapath (coming soon) depends on.

05 · Vocabulary

The Three Jobs Every ISA Must Do

Strip away the hundreds of mnemonics and every instruction ever designed does one of three jobs: compute, move data, or decide what runs next. These are exactly the CPI classes you mixed with sliders last lecture — now you meet them by name.

This slide is the bridge between the two lectures. Last week: "loads cost CPI 4, ALU ops cost 1" — abstract weights. Today: a load is lw, an ALU op is add. When we later count a program's instruction mix, you'll be counting these.
ClassMIPS examplesJobTypical CPI (last lecture!)
Arithmetic / logicadd, sub, addi, and, or, sltcompute in registers1
Data transferlw, sw, lb, sbregisters ⇄ memory3–4 (memory is far)
Control flowbeq, bne, j, jal, jrchoose the next instruction2 (+ stall risk)

add $t0,$s1,$s2

$t0 ← $s1 + $s2. Pure workbench: three registers, no memory, one cycle of ALU time.

lw $t0, 32($s3)

$t0 ← Memory[$s3 + 32]. A warehouse trip: compute an address, then wait for data to arrive.

beq $s3,$s4,L1

If $s3 == $s4, continue at label L1. The instruction that makes if, while, and for possible.

Real measured mixes (SPEC benchmarks on MIPS-class machines): roughly ~50% arithmetic, ~35% data transfer, ~15% control. Now the CPI slider defaults from last lecture stop being arbitrary — they were a real program's diet. A compiler that converts loads into register reuse is literally moving weight from the CPI-4 row to the CPI-1 row.
06 · Finding Data

Addressing Modes: base + offset

A load must name a memory address, but addresses are 32 bits and the instruction only spares 16. The MIPS answer: keep a base address in a register, put a small constant offset in the instruction, and add them. One mode covers variables, array elements, and struct fields.

It's a street address system: the register holds the building ($s0 = where the array starts) and the offset is the apartment number (which element). Because MIPS memory is byte-addressed and a word is 4 bytes, apartment i lives at offset 4 × i — forgetting the ×4 is this unit's classic exam wound.

THE MODES MIPS USES

Register (add $t0,$s1,$s2) · Immediate (addi $t0,$s1,4) · Base+displacement (lw $t0,32($s3)) · PC-relative (beq — branch target = PC + 4 + offset×4) · Pseudodirect (j — 26-bit field into the PC).

WHY SO FEW?

x86 offers exotic modes like base + index×scale + displacement in one instruction. Powerful — and slow to decode. MIPS bets that two simple instructions beat one complicated one. Hold that thought for two slides.

07 · The Discipline

Load/Store: Be Your Own Compiler

MIPS is a load/store architecture: only lw and sw may touch memory; arithmetic works on registers, period. So every line of C becomes a ritual — fetch operands to the bench, compute, put away the result. Let's compile by hand.

The ritual has three verbs: load what you need, work on the bench, store what you promised. Every hand-compilation problem on the exam is these three verbs plus the ×4 address math from the previous slide. Nothing else.

COMPILE THIS · variables: g→$s1, h→$s2, base of A→$s3

// C source A[12] = h + A[8];
A[8] is 8 words past the base → byte offset = 8 × 4 = 32. Fetch it to the bench: lw $t0, 32($s3)
Compute on registers only: add $t0, $s2, $t0  → $t0 now holds h + A[8]
A[12] → byte offset 12 × 4 = 48. Put the result away: sw $t0, 48($s3)
Done — one C statement became IC = 3 instructions: 2 memory ops + 1 ALU op. With last lecture's weights (lw=4, add=1, sw=3) that's 8 cycles of CPI budget for a single "=" sign.
Now you can see what an optimizing compiler earns its keep doing: if A[8] was already loaded two lines earlier and nothing changed it, the compiler skips the lw and reuses the register — deleting a CPI-4 instruction. Multiply that by a million-line program and "compiler flags" (-O2) stop being magic and start being arithmetic.
08 · Decisions

Branches: How if and while Are Built

There is no "if statement" in silicon. There is only beq / bne (branch if equal / not equal) and j (jump). Every if, else, while, and for in every language you know compiles down to these — usually with the test inverted.

The compiler's habit that confuses everyone: to compile "if (i == j) do X", it emits "bne … skip X". You branch around the code when the condition fails, and fall through into it when the condition holds. Read branches as "bail out if…" and assembly control flow becomes readable.

COMPILE THIS · f→$s0, g→$s1, h→$s2, i→$s3, j→$s4

// C source if (i == j) f = g + h; else f = g - h;
Invert the test — bail to Else when it fails: bne $s3, $s4, Else
Fall-through path (condition true): add $s0, $s1, $s2
True path must skip over the else code: j Exit
The other arm: Else: sub $s0, $s1, $s2  then  Exit: — labels are just names for addresses; the assembler turns them into PC-relative offsets (I-format) for you.
Total: IC = 4 instructions and one of them is a branch — the CPI-2, stall-risk class. When we study pipeline hazards, this exact pattern is where the bubbles will appear.
A while-loop is the same trick pointing backwards: test at the top, bne out when done, j back to the test. Your Unity game loop (MAC280 crowd), your Java for-loops (MAC286) — under every one of them, right now, is a conditional branch executed millions of times per second. Branch prediction hardware exists because loops make branches the most-repeated instructions on Earth.
09 · The Design War

RISC vs CISC: One Law, Two Bets

Should an ISA offer few, simple, fast instructions — or many, powerful, slow ones? This 45-year argument is just the Iron Law with two different strategies: CISC bets on shrinking IC; RISC bets on shrinking CPI × TC.

Recall the MIPS-trap slide: Compiler 1 used many simple instructions, Compiler 2 used few complex ones — and instruction count alone told you nothing. RISC vs CISC is that table turned into a philosophy of hardware design. Neither side "wins" by ideology; the Iron Law referees every match.
CISC (x86)RISC (MIPS, ARM, RISC-V)
Instruction length1–15 bytes, variable4 bytes, fixed
Memory accessmost instructions may touch memoryonly lw / sw

Iron Law betlower IC, tolerate higher CPIlower CPI & TC, tolerate higher IC
Decoder hardwarecomplex, power-hungrysmall, fast, pipeline-friendly

SETTLE IT WITH THE IRON LAW · same program, same 2.0 GHz clock

CISC machine: IC = 4×10⁹, CPI = 2.0  ·  RISC machine: IC = 8×10⁹, CPI = 0.8

TimeCISC = IC × CPI / f = 4×10⁹ × 2.0 / 2×10⁹ = 4.0 s
TimeRISC = 8×10⁹ × 0.8 / 2×10⁹ = 3.2 s — twice the instructions, still 20% faster
Speedup = 4.0 / 3.2 = 1.25×. The RISC machine executes more instructions and wins anyway — MIPS-the-metric would have called this backwards.
The plot twist of the modern era: x86 chips are secretly RISC inside. Since the mid-90s, Intel/AMD decoders translate CISC instructions into internal RISC-like micro-ops, then run those on a RISC-style core — paying a decoder tax on every instruction, forever. ARM (your phone, Apple M-series) and RISC-V (free & open, the academic darling) skip the tax. That's a big piece of why performance-per-watt now favors RISC designs — connect it to the power wall from last lecture.
10 · The Engine Turns

The Stored Program & the Fetch–Execute Cycle

Here is the deepest idea in computing: instructions are just numbers in memory, sitting beside the data. One register — the program counter (PC) — holds the address of the next one. Fetch it, decode it, execute it, add 4 to PC, repeat forever. That loop is your computer.

Every clock cycle you measured last lecture is spent somewhere inside this loop. Watch the PC below: it counts by 4, because every MIPS instruction is 4 bytes — the fixed-width promise from the formats slide is what makes "PC + 4" possible at all.

"Instructions are data" is why software exists at all: a compiler is a program whose output is instructions; an OS loads programs by copying numbers into memory and pointing PC at them. It's also why security is hard — a buffer overflow is an attacker sneaking numbers into memory and convincing PC to visit them. One idea, 80 years of consequences (von Neumann, 1945).
11 · Closing the Loop

Your Hands on the IC Knob

The Iron Law promised your code owns one knob. Now it's literal: every lw you avoid, every value you keep on the bench, every branch you restructure changes IC — and, through the instruction mix, CPI too. Same clock, same chip, faster program.

This is the whole reason an app developer should care about this course: the two programs below do the same job on the same machine. The only difference is how well their author speaks the ISA.
CPU Time= IC×CPI×TC  — today's lecture lives in the first two factors

NAIVE · reload everything, every time

# sum = A[0]+A[1], twice used lw $t0, 0($s3) lw $t1, 4($s3) add $t2, $t0, $t1 lw $t0, 0($s3) # reload! lw $t1, 4($s3) # reload! add $t3, $t0, $t1

IC = 6 · cycles = 4 lw × 4 + 2 add × 1 = 18 cycles

FLUENT · the bench remembers

# load once, reuse registers lw $t0, 0($s3) lw $t1, 4($s3) add $t2, $t0, $t1 add $t3, $t0, $t1

IC = 4 · cycles = 2 lw × 4 + 2 add × 1 = 10 cycles — a 1.8× speedup from literacy alone

Scale it up: at 3 GHz, saving 8 cycles per iteration of a loop that runs 10⁹ times returns 2.7 seconds — from editing four lines of assembly. This is the skill the rest of the semester trains: next we build the datapath that executes these instructions, then pipeline it, and suddenly the stalls and hazards will happen to code you can read.
12 · Checkpoint

Prove It — Quick Checkpoint

Mental math only. Click an answer for instant feedback.

Each question maps to one slide: Q1 → formats, Q2 → anatomy, Q3 → addressing, Q4 → registers, Q5 → RISC vs CISC via the Iron Law. Miss one → revisit that slide and play with its instrument until the answer feels obvious.
Q1 · How long is a MIPS instruction?
Every MIPS instruction is one 32-bit word — the fixed-width promise. That's why PC always advances by 4 and why decoding is fast. (x86 is the variable-length counterexample.)
Q2 · After add $t0, $s1, $s2, which register changed?
Destination first: $t0 ← $s1 + $s2. Sources are only read, never written.
Q3 · lw $t0, 12($s0) with $s0 = 2000. What address is read?
Effective address = base + offset = 2000 + 12 = 2012. (And if that 12 was meant to be "element 12", the programmer wanted offset 48 — the ×4 trap!)
Q4 · What does $zero hold after you write 7 into it?
$zero is physically wired to 0. It exists so common patterns (copy, compare-to-zero, load small constant) don't need special instructions.
Q5 · Same program, same 2 GHz clock. CISC: IC = 4×10⁹, CPI = 2.0. RISC: IC = 8×10⁹, CPI = 0.8. Which is faster?
Iron Law: CISC = 4×10⁹×2.0/2×10⁹ = 4.0 s; RISC = 8×10⁹×0.8/2×10⁹ = 3.2 s. Instruction count alone is the MIPS trap all over again.
